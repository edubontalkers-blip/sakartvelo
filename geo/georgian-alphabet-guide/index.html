Georgian Alphabet: History and How to Read It as a Tourist

🇬🇪 Georgia Travel Guide  ·  Published: July 10, 2026  ·  sakartvelo.ai

Walking through Tbilisi, you'll immediately notice something striking: the signs, menus, and street names are written in a curving, almost musical script unlike anything you've seen before. This is the Georgian alphabet — one of the world's most distinctive and ancient writing systems, and learning even a little about it will transform your experience in the country.

Georgia actually uses not one but three related scripts, all developed over centuries. The oldest, Asomtavruli, dates back to at least the 5th century AD, with inscriptions found in ancient churches throughout the country. The second, Nuskhuri, emerged as a church script used alongside it. The third and most widely used today is Mkhedruli, which developed around the 11th century and became the standard script for everyday writing. In 2016, all three scripts together were inscribed on the UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage list — a recognition of their extraordinary cultural significance and uniqueness.

The Georgian script has 33 letters, each representing a single sound. Unlike Arabic or Hebrew, it is written left to right. Unlike Latin or Cyrillic, it has no uppercase and lowercase distinction in Mkhedruli — every letter is the same size. The alphabet was likely created or formalized to give the Georgian language and the newly Christianized kingdom its own literary identity, independent of Greek or Armenian influence.

For tourists, reading Georgian script can feel intimidating at first, but it's actually more phonetic and consistent than English. Every letter makes one sound, every sound has one letter. Once you learn the characters, pronunciation is straightforward. Several free apps and online resources can teach you the basics in just a few hours. Even memorizing ten or fifteen letters will help you decode menus, recognize station names on the Tbilisi Metro, and navigate street signs with much more confidence.

If you want to go deeper, the Georgian National Museum in Tbilisi (located on Rustaveli Avenue, entry around 15 GEL for adults) displays ancient manuscripts and inscriptions that bring the alphabet's history vividly to life. The Sioni Cathedral and Jvari Monastery also feature early Georgian inscriptions worth seeking out. In Mtskheta, just 20 kilometers from Tbilisi and easily reached by marshrutka for about 1–2 GEL, you can see some of the oldest surviving examples of the Georgian script carved into church stone.

Many souvenir shops sell beautifully designed prints and jewelry featuring the Georgian alphabet — a meaningful gift that connects directly to this UNESCO-recognized heritage. You can find these in the Dry Bridge Market in Tbilisi or along Shardeni Street in the Old Town.

One charming practical note: Georgians are genuinely delighted when visitors make any effort to learn their script. Asking a local to write your name in Georgian — or having it printed on a small card — is a lovely, memorable interaction that costs nothing but creates a real connection.

The Georgian alphabet is not just a writing system. It is a symbol of national identity, resilience, and cultural pride that has survived over fifteen centuries. Understanding even a fraction of its story will give you a richer, more respectful appreciation of the country you are exploring.
