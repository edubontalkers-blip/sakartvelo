Georgian Food: A Complete Guide for Tourists Visiting Georgia

🇬🇪 Georgia Travel Guide  ·  Published: June 27, 2026  ·  sakartvelo.ai

Georgia, the small but extraordinary country nestled in the Caucasus, is quietly becoming one of the world's most exciting food destinations. With a culinary tradition stretching back thousands of years, Georgian cuisine is bold, generous, and deeply rooted in culture. If you are planning a trip, food alone is a reason to visit.

Georgian cuisine draws on influences from the Middle East, Eastern Europe, and Central Asia, yet remains entirely its own. Walnuts, pomegranate, fresh herbs, garlic, and the iconic spice blend Khmeli Suneli appear across countless dishes. Meals here are not rushed affairs — they are celebrations.

No visit to Georgia is complete without trying Khinkali, the country's beloved dumplings. These twisted, juicy parcels are typically filled with spiced meat — usually pork and beef — though mushroom and cheese versions are popular for vegetarians. The technique for eating them matters: hold the dumpling by its thick doughy knob, bite a small hole, sip the hot broth inside, then eat the rest. The knob is traditionally left on the plate. In Tbilisi, a portion of five Khinkali at a local restaurant costs roughly 5 to 8 Georgian Lari, making them one of the best budget meals in the country.

Khachapuri is arguably Georgia's most famous dish internationally, and for good reason. This cheese-filled bread comes in several regional varieties. Adjarian Khachapuri, shaped like a boat and topped with a raw egg and butter, originates from the Black Sea region and is visually stunning. Imeruli Khachapuri is a flatter, round version stuffed generously with Imereti cheese. In Tbilisi cafes and bakeries, you can find Khachapuri for as little as 6 to 12 Lari depending on the type and location.

Beyond individual dishes, understanding the Supra is essential for any visitor hoping to connect with Georgian culture. A Supra is a traditional Georgian feast, presided over by a Tamada, or toastmaster, who guides the table through elaborate, heartfelt toasts covering themes like life, love, family, and the homeland. Georgians say there is no Supra without wine, and the country produces some of the world's oldest wine, made using the ancient Qvevri clay vessel method recognized by UNESCO. If a local family invites you to a Supra, accept without hesitation — it is an unforgettable experience.

Other traditional food highlights worth seeking out include Pkhali, finely chopped vegetables mixed with walnut paste and spices and shaped into small balls, and Lobiani, a flatbread stuffed with seasoned kidney beans. For meat lovers, Mtsvadi — Georgian skewered grilled meat — is a must, especially prepared over a wood fire.

Practical tips for food lovers visiting Georgia: the Deserter's Market in Tbilisi is a wonderful place to explore local cheeses, spices, fresh bread, and produce. The Dezerter Bazaar opens daily from early morning. For a sit-down meal in a mid-range restaurant in Tbilisi, expect to spend around 25 to 50 Lari per person including wine. Tipping is not mandatory but leaving 10 percent is appreciated.

Georgia rewards curious eaters at every turn. Whether you are slurping Khinkali at a roadside canteen or raising a glass of amber wine at a Supra, the food of Georgia is generous, soulful, and genuinely unforgettable.
